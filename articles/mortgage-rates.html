Mortgage Repayments at Different Interest Rates: 3%, 4%, 5%, 6% and 7% Compared

The same £200,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years can produce very different monthly repayments depending on the interest rate attached to it.

The examples below use hypothetical rates of 3%, 4%, 5%, 6% and 7% purely to illustrate the effect of the rate on repayments. They are not today's mortgage rates, and they aren't a prediction of future ones.

Mortgage payments at 3%, 4%, 5%, 6% and 7%

Assumptions: £200,000 repayment (capital & interest) mortgage, 25-year term, interest rate held constant for the full term, no fees, no overpayments and no rate changes — a standard monthly amortising repayment calculation. A real borrower wouldn't necessarily keep the same rate for the full 25 years; this is illustrative only.

Rate Monthly repayment Annual repayment Total interest (25 yrs) Monthly vs 3%
3% £948.42 £11,381 £84,527 —
4% £1,055.67 £12,668 £116,702 +£107.25
5% £1,169.18 £14,030 £150,754 +£220.76
6% £1,288.60 £15,463 £186,581 +£340.18
7% £1,413.56 £16,963 £224,068 +£465.14

£200,000 repayment mortgage, 25-year term. Figures are rounded for display; see assumptions above.

At 3%, the monthly repayment is £948.42. Moving to 4% adds about £107 a month — roughly £1,287 more a year. At 5%, the gap widens to around £221 a month more than at 3%, and total interest over the full term rises from £84,527 to £150,754 — an extra £66,227 over 25 years from the higher rate alone. At 7%, the monthly repayment is £1,413.56 — £465 more than at 3% — and total interest more than doubles, to £224,068.

What does a 1% higher mortgage rate actually cost?

The effect of an extra percentage point isn't a fixed £ amount — it depends on the outstanding balance, the mortgage term and the starting rate. On our £200,000, 25-year example, each extra point actually costs progressively more in cash terms:

  • 3% → 4%: about +£107 a month
  • 4% → 5%: about +£114 a month more
  • 5% → 6%: about +£119 a month more
  • 6% → 7%: about +£125 a month more

Moving from 3% to 4% is not the same £ change as moving from 6% to 7%, even though both are "1%" moves — the step gets a little more expensive each time. A larger balance or a shorter term would change these figures further, which is why it's worth calculating your own numbers rather than assuming a flat rule of thumb.

£100,000 £584.59 £75,377

25-year term, 5% interest rate, for illustration only.

The same 5% rate produces very different cash effects depending on the size of the mortgage — doubling the balance from £100,000 to £200,000 roughly doubles both the monthly repayment and the total interest. The rate itself hasn't changed; what changes is how much it's being applied to.

Why do higher interest rates increase mortgage repayments?

A repayment mortgage payment is made up of two parts: interest on the amount you still owe, and capital that actually reduces what you owe. When the interest rate is higher, more interest is charged on that outstanding balance each month — so the payment required to clear the mortgage within the same term has to be higher too.

In the earlier years of a repayment mortgage, a larger share of each payment typically goes toward interest rather than capital, simply because the outstanding balance — and therefore the interest charged on it — is at its highest early on. As the balance falls, more of each payment goes toward capital instead, even though the payment amount itself stays the same for the rest of a fixed-rate deal.

What if you're already on a fixed-rate mortgage?

With a fixed-rate mortgage, the rate you've agreed normally stays fixed for the length of that deal, so general market or Bank Rate movements don't usually change your payment while you're still within the fixed period.

Rate differences become particularly relevant when the fixed deal ends, when you move onto a new mortgage product, when you remortgage, or if you move onto your lender's standard variable rate (SVR). At that point, the rate available to you — whatever it happens to be — determines your new repayment, in much the same way the illustrative rates above determine repayments in this article.

This article can't tell you which mortgage product or rate is right for you — that depends on your own circumstances and is worth discussing with a mortgage adviser or your lender. For a general explanation of how mortgage interest rates work, MoneyHelper's guide on understanding mortgages and interest rates is a good starting point.

What about tracker and variable-rate mortgages?

A tracker mortgage's rate is set to follow a specified underlying rate — commonly the Bank of England's Bank Rate — plus a fixed margin set by the lender. A standard variable rate (SVR), by contrast, is a rate the lender sets itself and can change more or less at its own discretion, within its terms.

Because these rates can move, the monthly payment on a tracker or variable-rate mortgage can change whenever the applicable rate changes — unlike a fixed-rate mortgage, which is insulated from those movements until the fixed period ends. MoneyHelper explains this in more detail in how will interest rates affect my mortgage?, and the Bank of England's own explainer on what are interest rates? covers how Bank Rate itself works.

Frequently asked questions

How much difference does 1% make to a mortgage?

It depends on the outstanding balance, term and starting rate — it isn't a fixed amount. On a £200,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years, our example shows each extra percentage point adding roughly £107 to £125 a month, with the size of the increase itself growing at higher rates.

Does a higher mortgage rate always mean a higher monthly payment?

For a standard repayment mortgage with the same balance and term, yes — a higher interest rate increases the monthly repayment needed to clear the loan over that term, because more interest is being charged on the outstanding balance.

Will my mortgage payment change if interest rates change?

It depends on your mortgage type. A fixed-rate mortgage's payment normally stays the same for the fixed deal period regardless of wider rate movements. A tracker or variable-rate mortgage's payment can change whenever the rate it follows changes.

Does a fixed-rate mortgage change when the Bank of England changes rates?

Not during the fixed period — the rate you've agreed is normally held for the length of the deal. Bank Rate movements become relevant again once that fixed period ends, for example when you remortgage or move onto your lender's standard variable rate.

What happens to a tracker mortgage when rates change?

A tracker mortgage's rate is set to follow a specified underlying rate, commonly the Bank of England's Bank Rate, plus a fixed margin. When that underlying rate changes, the tracker rate — and therefore the monthly payment — normally changes with it.
